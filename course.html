THE SIFWAKU METHOD / THINKING & REASONING

The complete
reasoning process.

The Sifwaku Method is a complete reasoning process designed to help a person think more clearly, solve problems systematically, evaluate information responsibly, and make better decisions. It begins with awareness of how you are thinking, moves through observation, problem definition, questioning, evidence and logic, and ends with responsible action and the willingness to revise a conclusion when better information becomes available.

01 / METACOGNITIONNotice how you know, assume, and decide.

Become aware of your own thinking process. Understand how you form beliefs, interpret information, make assumptions, and arrive at conclusions.

02 / OBSERVATIONSeparate what happened from what you think it means.

Distinguish observable facts from interpretations, opinions, explanations, and assumptions. First establish what is known before deciding what it means.

03 / PROBLEM FRAMINGDefine the real problem, its scope, and its desired outcome.

Clarify what actually needs to be solved. Identify the boundaries of the problem, what is within your control, and what a successful outcome would look like.

04 / QUESTIONINGAsk questions that reveal meaning and evidence.

Use precise and useful questions to uncover information, clarify uncertainty, challenge assumptions, and understand the problem more deeply.

05 / DECOMPOSITIONBreak a complex problem into workable parts.

Take a large or complicated problem and divide it into smaller components that can be understood, investigated, and solved individually.

06 / ASSUMPTIONSMake hidden beliefs visible and testable.

Identify the things you are taking for granted. Ask whether those assumptions are supported by evidence and determine what would happen if they were wrong.

07 / CRITICAL THINKINGEvaluate claims, evidence, reasoning, and conclusions.

Examine information rather than accepting it automatically. Consider whether a claim is supported, whether the reasoning is sound, and whether the conclusion follows from the available evidence.

08 / LOGICCheck whether reasons actually support a conclusion.

Examine the structure of an argument. Determine whether the premises provide sufficient support for the conclusion and identify contradictions, invalid reasoning, or logical fallacies.

09 / EVIDENCEJudge source quality, relevance, and corroboration.

Consider where information comes from, how reliable the source is, whether the evidence is relevant to the claim, and whether independent evidence supports it.

10 / CAUSAL THINKINGDistinguish association from what actually produces change.

Do not assume that because two things occur together, one caused the other. Examine mechanisms, alternative explanations, and evidence of cause and effect.

11 / SYSTEMSSee relationships, feedback, and delayed effects.

Look beyond individual events and examine how different parts interact. Consider feedback loops, dependencies, unintended consequences, and effects that may appear later.

12 / BIASRecognize predictable distortions and fallacies.

Identify cognitive biases, emotional influences, misleading framing, and common reasoning errors that can distort how information is interpreted or decisions are made.

13 / STATISTICSReason about variation, samples, and uncertainty.

Use statistical thinking to understand patterns in data without overstating what the data can prove. Consider variation, sample size, probability, uncertainty, and the difference between signal and noise.

14 / DECISIONSChoose actions with trade-offs and risk in view.

Use the available evidence to compare possible actions. Consider benefits, costs, risks, constraints, opportunity costs, and potential consequences before acting.

15 / HUMILITYKnow what would change your mind.

Recognize the limits of your current knowledge. Remain willing to update your position when stronger evidence or better reasoning becomes available.

THE COMPLETE PROCESS

Metacognition → Observation → Problem Framing → Questioning → Decomposition → Assumptions → Critical Thinking → Logic → Evidence → Causal Thinking → Systems → Bias → Statistics → Decisions → Humility

The goal is not simply to think more. It is to think deliberately, test what you believe, understand why you believe it, act responsibly, and remain willing to learn.
